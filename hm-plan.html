    Foundation

      Effort guide

      The plan runs on how a run feels rather than a fixed pace, so it adapts as you get fitter.

      Easy
      Conversational the whole way: you could talk in full sentences. Most of the plan, including every long run. If you're breathing hard, slow down.
      Tempo
      Comfortably hard. You can speak in short phrases but not full sentences. Teaches your body to hold a faster pace for longer.
      Race-pace
      The effort you plan to hold for all 21.1 km. Controlled and sustainable, never a sprint. You're rehearsing race day, not testing your limit.
      Hills
      Short repeats up a moderate slope at a strong effort, jogging or walking back down. Builds leg strength and running form.
      Strides
      4–6 relaxed accelerations of 15–20 s after an easy run, up to a quick pace and back down. They sharpen form without adding real fatigue.

      Strength & cross-training

      The Wednesday session matters more than it looks. Strong hips and glutes are the best insurance against runner's knee, IT band irritation and shin pain, the injuries that tend to appear as weekly distance climbs.

      Strength, bodyweight, 20–25 min

        Cross-training, 30–40 min

        Cycling, swimming or the elliptical on the alternate Wednesdays. Aerobic fitness without impact, useful in a week when your legs feel flat.

        Fuel & recovery

        Training adaptations happen during rest, not during the run. Sleep is the highest-leverage part of this plan.

        Everyday eating

        Regular meals with carbohydrate, protein and some colour at each one. As weekly distance rises in phases 2 and 3, appetite usually rises too. That's your body asking for fuel, so don't restrict.

        On long runs

        Under an hour, water is enough. Once long runs pass about 60–75 minutes, practise race nutrition: a small carbohydrate source (gel, chews or diluted juice) every 40–45 minutes, with water. Use the exact same fuel and timing in your last few long runs so race day has no surprises.

        Iron and vitamin D

        Needs run a little higher in endurance training, particularly for women. If tiredness persists and rest doesn't fix it, mention it to a doctor and get levels checked rather than guessing.

        Staying injury-free

        • Normal fatigue vs pain: tired legs and mild, general soreness are expected. Sharp, one-sided or joint-specific pain is not. Stop, rest a day or two, and scale back if it doesn't settle.
        • Respect the recovery weeks: the lighter weeks aren't optional extras. They're when your body absorbs the training.
        • Warm up before quality sessions: a few minutes of easy jogging plus leg swings and walking lunges before tempo, hill or race-pace runs.
        • Shoes: replace them every 500–700 km. Worn-out cushioning is a common, avoidable cause of niggles.
        • New to running shoes or have a gait issue? A short visit to a specialist running shop or physio early in phase 1 costs far less than an injury in phase 3.

        Race week playbook

        The taper is training too

        Running less in the final three weeks feels strange, but it's what turns months of work into race-day fitness. You aren't losing fitness by resting.

        The 2–3 days before

        Shift your plate a little towards carbohydrate, keep drinking, and lay out your kit and bib the night before so race morning is boring and predictable.

        On the start line

        Start slower than feels necessary; the first 5K should feel almost too easy. A conservative first half and a stronger second half beats the reverse every time. Think in 5K blocks and keep the fuel rhythm you rehearsed. The "easy start" splits in the pace calculator follow this.

        After the finish

        Keep walking for 10–15 minutes, refuel with carbohydrate and protein within an hour, and give the next few days to gentle movement like walking or an easy spin.

        Race kit checklist

        This plan is general training guidance, not medical advice. Check with a doctor before starting a new training programme, especially with an existing injury or health condition.

        If a session doesn't fit your week, move it rather than giving up your only rest day. Consistency over months matters far more than any single run.
