About this plan

You can already run 5K slowly without stopping — that's a real base, and it means there's no need to rush. With 28 weeks on the calendar, this plan takes the conservative route: small, steady increases in distance, a genuine recovery week roughly every third or fourth week, and no jump bigger than your legs can absorb. The goal is simple — arrive at the start line healthy, not just fit.

Pace & effort guide

This plan is built around how a run feels rather than a fixed pace, since that adapts automatically as your fitness improves.

Easy

Conversational the whole way — you could talk in full sentences. This is most of the plan, including every long run. If you're breathing hard, slow down; easy runs build endurance precisely because they don't tax you.

Tempo

Comfortably hard — you could speak in short phrases, not full sentences. These sessions teach your body to hold a faster pace for longer without tipping into exhaustion.

Race-pace

The effort you plan to hold for all 21.1 km on race day. It should feel controlled and sustainable, never like a sprint — you're rehearsing race day, not testing your limit.

Strides

4–6 relaxed accelerations of 15–20 seconds after an easy run, up to a quick pace and back down. They sharpen running form without adding real fatigue.

Strength & cross-training

The Wednesday session matters more than it looks. Strong hips and glutes are the single best insurance policy against the injuries — runner's knee, IT band irritation, shin pain — that tend to show up when weekly mileage climbs.

Strength (bodyweight, 20–25 min)

  • Squats and reverse lunges — 3 sets of 10–12 per side
  • Glute bridges or single-leg bridges — 3 sets of 12
  • Side-lying leg raises or banded clamshells — 2 sets of 15 per side
  • Plank and side plank — 3 rounds of 30–45 seconds
  • Single-leg balance, eyes closed if it's easy — 3 x 30 seconds per side

Cross-training (30–40 min)

Cycling, swimming, or an elliptical session on the alternate Wednesdays. The aim is aerobic fitness without impact — useful on a week where your legs feel flat.

Fuel & recovery

Training adaptations happen during rest, not during the run itself — sleep is arguably the highest-leverage part of this plan.

Everyday eating

Aim for regular meals with a source of carbohydrate, protein, and colour at each one. As weekly mileage rises through Phases 2 and 3, appetite usually rises with it — that's your body asking for more fuel, not a signal to restrict.

On long runs

For runs under an hour, water is enough. Once long runs pass about 60–75 minutes, start practising race nutrition: a small carbohydrate source (gel, chews, or diluted juice) every 40–45 minutes, with water. Whatever you land on, use the exact same fuel and timing in your last few long runs so race day has no surprises.

A note for endurance athletes

Iron and vitamin D needs run a little higher in endurance training, particularly for women. If you notice persistent fatigue that rest doesn't fix, it's worth mentioning to a doctor and getting levels checked rather than guessing.

Staying injury-free

  • Normal fatigue vs. pain: tired legs and mild, general soreness are expected. A sharp, one-sided, or joint-specific pain is not — stop, rest a day or two, and scale back if it doesn't settle.
  • Respect the recovery weeks: the lighter weeks built into this plan aren't optional extras — they're when your body actually absorbs the training.
  • Warm up before quality sessions: a few minutes of easy jogging plus leg swings and walking lunges before tempo, hill, or race-pace runs.
  • Shoes: plan on a replacement every 500–700 km — worn-out cushioning is a common, avoidable cause of niggles.
  • New to running shoes or a gait issue? A short visit to a specialist running shop or physiotherapist early in Phase 1 is far cheaper than an injury in Phase 3.

Race week playbook

The taper is training too

Running less in the final three weeks feels strange, but it's what lets weeks of hard work convert into race-day fitness. Trust it — you are not losing fitness by resting.

The 2–3 days before

Shift your plate slightly more towards carbohydrates, keep hydrating, and lay out your kit and bib the night before so race morning is boring and predictable.

On the start line

Start slower than feels necessary — the first 5K should feel almost too easy. A conservative first half and a stronger second half beats the reverse every time. Break the race into 5K blocks mentally, and stick to the fuelling rhythm you rehearsed in training.

After you cross the line

Keep walking for 10–15 minutes rather than stopping abruptly, refuel with carbohydrate and protein within an hour, and expect the next few days to call for gentle movement — walking or an easy spin — rather than another run.

This plan is general training guidance, not medical advice — check with a doctor before starting a new training programme, especially with any existing injury or health condition. If a session doesn't fit your week, shift it rather than skip your only rest day; consistency over months matters far more than any single run.
